Interview reviews reveal applicants’ experiences of advertised jobs that appear unavailable.

Main validation: separate reviews

Higher ghost-job scores point to more unavailable-role reports in other reviews: about 5.6%, versus 3.6%.

Imagine 1,000 reviews from people who did not receive an offer. In higher-scoring company-years, about 56 describe an unavailable role; in lower-scoring company-years, about 36 do. That is about 20 extra reports per 1,000 reviews.

The score comes from a different group of reviews. This supports our measure because it points to experiences reported beyond the accounts used to build it.

Job ads and later growth: the Lourie check

Gutiérrez, Lourie, Nekrasov and Shevlin (2020) find that more active job ads predict later hiring, spending, sales and earnings. We use this as a benchmark: first check that ads predict growth, then ask whether that link is weaker at companies with more ghost-job reports.

The corrected broad sample reproduces three results: more ads predict higher employment, operating spending and sales. All three remain statistically clear after accounting for four outcomes. The earnings result remains unclear.

Our original scraped review score can be added for a much smaller group of companies. In that original-score group, the expected weakening is still uncertain. The larger Revelio-review test is reported below. Establishing the broad posting pattern is the first step; it does not by itself validate the ghost score.

Why the earlier sample was small—and what was corrected

The earlier posting benchmark was unnecessarily limited to the old review-linked financial file and December year ends. We now use the broader financial file already on hand and each company’s actual fiscal year. We corrected company links, including ticker collisions and advertisements assigned to today’s parent before an acquisition. We also corrected the book-equity calculation and removed a five-review cutoff that was not required by the original specification.

The full spending sample increased from 455 company-years at 74 companies to 6,406 at 1,107 companies. With our original saved review score, it increased from 340 company-years at 68 companies to 520 at 86. These figures reflect several corrections together.

Millions of ads do not mean millions of independent regression observations: each company and year contributes one row. Requiring disclosed R&D and advertising spending removes many rows. For the broad spending test, 20,962 company-years have the core controls; adding R&D leaves 13,649, and adding advertising leaves 6,406. With the original review score, the corresponding counts are 1,266, 877 and 520. Missing expenses are not treated as zero.

For a December-end company, we compare average daily open ads in January–March with January–March a year earlier, then examine operating growth during the following full financial year. Other companies use the corresponding fiscal months. The saved review-score test keeps December year ends so its calendar-year score lines up; a separate check rebuilds fiscal-period shares from unchanged original labels.

What the classification, company and timing audit found

The score mainly identifies reported communication problems. About 84% of flags in the spending-test sample concern lost contact or ghosting without direct unavailable-role or internal-hire reports. A company can fill real jobs while ignoring many unsuccessful applicants; a high review score therefore need not weaken the job-ad-to-growth link.

Interview dates, when supplied, usually identify a month. Reviews often arrive months later. We checked interview-month timing, the first-quarter recruiting window, U.S. reviews, stricter company links, matched root-employer ads and company effects. None of the 148 interaction terms in 288 diagnostic fits gives a significant negative result. Removing each of 1,025 companies in turn also leaves the spending interaction’s point estimate positive.

The literal word rule can wrongly flag “I wasn’t ghosted.” Even removing all 49 possible negation cases leaves the result unclear. Some auxiliary company IDs disagree, while independent names and financial identifiers corroborate the largest examples; stricter matching does not produce the expected weakening.

The separate-applicant confirmation above is useful measurement evidence. The corporate-growth attenuation prediction remains unsupported by these checks. All variants are exploratory and retained; no favorable subset replaces the primary test.

Interview rounds: what the result means

More reported interview activity is followed by higher operating spending over two years. That link is weaker at companies with higher ghost-job scores.

Lourie and coauthors find that more job advertisements predict later company growth. We borrow that logic, using the total interview rounds described across reviews as a sign of recruitment activity.

A hypothetical example: two otherwise similar companies each increase their total reported interview rounds from 100 to 150. Company A has a low ghost-job score and later shows a larger rise in operating spending. Company B has a high score and shows a smaller rise. These numbers illustrate the idea; they are not actual observations or a numerical forecast.

Why it supports our measure: higher scores identify companies where recruitment activity has a weaker connection to later operations—the pattern we would expect if some recruitment does not represent genuine hiring opportunities.

This is supporting, exploratory evidence. Operating spending includes administration, selling and other overhead, rather than hiring costs alone. Removing any one of the 150 companies keeps both expected directions, but some model changes make the result less clear. The separate-review check remains our main validation.

Robustness results and what they mean

We examined 34 settings, producing 68 regressions, plus 408 checks that omit each company, industry or year in turn. The original fit has 749 company-years from 150 companies. Its bootstrap p-values are .040 for the positive spending link and .033 for the negative ghost interaction. These resampling checks assess the selected model; they do not undo the earlier search.

Controlling for the number of reviews preserves the interaction. Allowing review-volume growth itself to interact with the ghost score makes it less precise. Company effects, average rounds per review, and pandemic-free windows do not clearly reproduce the full pattern. Total rounds also increase when more people write reviews, so this does not establish that longer interview processes drive the result.

Five of 33 forward-outcome settings show both expected relationships before adjustment, including the original fit and closely related round-cap choices. None survives adjustment that includes the earlier search. We describe it as supplementary economic evidence, rather than established validation or an exact posting-based replication. Actual dated advertisement counts remain the missing input for the original Lourie design.

How we identify them

We score reports of unavailable roles, hiring freezes, internal hiring and lost contact. Rejection alone is not enough. Applicants who received offers remain in the sample and are counted as non-ghost experiences. Automated labels; human review pending. Full scoring rule.

What the reviews reveal

The flagged accounts describe hiring put on hold, roles reserved for future hiring, internal candidates receiving the job, and communication stopping after interviews.
